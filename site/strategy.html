1. The chain

You bet a small amount, say 10, with Auto Cash Out at 1.90×. If the plane flies past 1.90×, the game pays you 19.00. Instead of taking that money, you bet all of it on the next round. Another win pays 36.10, and that rides too. That is compounding: each win becomes the next stake.

After a set number of wins in a row (here five), the chain is complete: 10 has become 247.60, a profit of 237.60, and you start a fresh chain from 10. If any round in the chain crashes before 1.90×, the chain is over and you have lost only your 10. Everything above it was the chain's own winnings. Then a new chain starts from 10.

That is the whole appeal: a small, fixed risk per chain, and a big payout when a chain completes.

3. Why the odds shrink as fast as the stake grows

Each step needs one more win. At 1.90× a single bet wins about 51% of the time ({{rtpPercent}} ÷ 1.90). Two wins in a row happen about 26% of the time, five in a row about 3.5%: roughly once in 29 chains. So the stake grows by ×1.9 per step, and the chance of getting there falls by about ×0.51 per step.

Every round is independent. The plane doesn't remember the last round, so a long run of low crashes doesn't make a high one "due", and a win doesn't make the next win more likely.

4. The house edge compounds too

The casino states that this game returns {{rtpPercent}} of what is bet. On average every bet loses {{edgePercent}} of its stake, at any multiplier. A five-step chain is five bets riding on each other, so on average it returns {{rtpPercent}} × {{rtpPercent}} × {{rtpPercent}} × {{rtpPercent}} × {{rtpPercent}} ≈ 86% of its first bet. In other words it loses about 14% per chain on average.

That's the honest summary of compounding. It changes the shape of your results, not their direction. Most chains lose their first bet, a few win big, and the average stays below zero. In savings, compound interest grows money because every period adds a positive return. Here every round has a small negative return, so compounding multiplies that too.

5. What each setting changes

Cash out at (multiplier)
Lower wins more often but grows slowly; higher grows fast but rarely completes a chain. The average return per bet is the same at any multiplier.
Wins per chain
Each extra step multiplies the payout by your multiplier and the chance by about one half. It also multiplies the average return by {{rtpPercent}} once more.
Loss limit
Each lost chain costs exactly one initial bet, so a limit of 10 bets allows 10 lost chains. It's the most important setting: it decides the most you can lose in a session.
Stop after (completed chains)
Ends the session after a win instead of giving it back. With 1, the session stops at the first completed chain.
Time limit
Ends the session after a set time, whatever happens. Breaks matter.
Rounds between bets
Waits a random number of rounds after each bet. It spreads bets out over time; it doesn't change the odds of any bet.

6. Compounding vs Martingale

Martingale doubles the bet after every loss to win back what was lost. It wins small amounts often, but a losing streak demands huge bets, and one bad run wipes out everything. Compounding is the reverse: it raises the stake only with money the chain has won, so new money is risked one initial bet at a time. That makes losses slow and capped. It doesn't turn a losing game into a winning one.

7. Myths to ignore

  • "Predictors" and "signals". Rounds are decided by the game's random generator before they start. Anyone selling predictions is selling a scam.
  • "Patterns" in the history. Past crash points tell you nothing about the next one.
  • "The right settings always win." No setting changes the {{edgePercent}} average loss per bet. Settings only change how wins and losses are spread.

8. Knowing when to stop

Decide what you can afford to lose before you start and make it your loss limit. Set a time limit. Never raise the limit to chase losses. If gambling stops being fun, take a break, and see our responsible gambling page for help.
